SLIDE 1 — Local AI Coach

What if you could teach an AI a job once, then have it work on your own computer? That is the idea behind Local AI Coach. You bring the task and examples of what good work looks like. A stronger AI helps configure a smaller local model, and the app checks its work. Our vision is a personal AI worker that follows your rules. Our working demo starts with survey feedback.

SLIDE 2 — Feedback arrives.
Someone still has to read it.

Imagine Maya, who runs a neighborhood café. Her survey collects feedback about the drinks, prices, service, and space. She wants to understand the patterns and catch comments that need follow-up. But a single response can contain praise and criticism about several things. Maya is a fictional example, but she illustrates the workflow we are designing for: repeated decisions that depend on someone’s own categories and judgment.

SLIDE 3 — A teacher for your local AI

The parent AI acts as a coach. It looks at a development subset of the local model’s answers alongside human labels, identifies mistakes, and proposes better rules. You review those rules. The small model then runs locally with the approved instructions. Today, that changes the prompt rather than the model’s weights. The crucial part is the check afterward: a suggestion has to earn our confidence through comparison.

SLIDE 4 — A complete feedback loop

Let me show you the loop. We upload a CSV, map the response and label columns, and run the original local model. The app shows where its predictions disagree with the labels. With permission, the parent analyzes a development subset and proposes changes. We review the rules and compare the revised configuration on reserved responses. The original run remains available, and the results can be exported.

SLIDE 5 — The lesson behind a mistake

Here is an example of the kind of lesson the system can propose. A comment praises the coffee and criticizes the service. Suppose the local model selects only drink quality and calls it positive. The parent can propose two explicit instructions: include every topic the response mentions, and use mixed sentiment for praise and criticism together. We then check the revised instructions on other responses. This illustrates the mechanism, rather than claiming a measured improvement.

SLIDE 6 — A useful local baseline

Our original local model already does useful work. On the fictional survey dataset, the reported baseline achieved 77.6 percent topic micro-F1, 71.7 percent sentiment accuracy, and 90 percent review-flag accuracy. Schema validity was 98.3 percent. That gives us a concrete starting point and real mistakes to inspect. These numbers describe the original model. We are not claiming an improvement from the parent until the reserved comparison has been completed and verified.

SLIDE 7 — A local worker for repeated work

We would start with small teams that repeatedly sort open-ended feedback using their own categories: café owners, event teams, and small research groups. The proposed business model is a subscription for configuring, evaluating, and managing those workers, with parent-model usage priced separately. Our first routes to users would be campus organizations and local business networks. Those are our initial market and pricing hypotheses, rather than claims of existing customers.

SLIDE 8 — Teach it once. Put it to work.

Survey Cleaner is the first example of a broader idea. We want a person to describe a recurring job, show examples, and leave with a local worker whose behavior they can inspect. Today, we have CSV evaluation, parent rule proposals, and the comparison workflow. The next product layer is a portable configuration and connections such as Google Sheets. Longer term, optional adapter training could change the model itself when better instructions are not enough.

SLIDE 9 — Local AI Coach

The future we want is one where a useful AI can run on your own computer, follow your rules, and show you how well it is doing. Local AI Coach is our first step toward that: a stronger AI helping a smaller one become useful for your work. You define good work. The parent proposes the lesson. The local worker does the job, and the results stay open to inspection.

JUDGE Q&A

Why not use a cloud model for every response?
That can be the right choice. We are designing for recurring jobs where a smaller local model can do useful work, with a stronger parent helping configure it. The product makes the quality tradeoff visible. We have not yet established cost or speed savings.

Is this training?
Today it is instruction optimization and evaluation. Optional adapter training is part of the longer-term vision.

Does all the data stay local?
The worker runs locally. With consent, the parent receives up to 20 development excerpts, their human labels and predictions, plus the task context, taxonomy and rules. The comparison responses and unrelated CSV columns are not sent to the parent. Do not promise that everything stays on the laptop or that excerpts are anonymized.

How do you know a change helps?
The app compares original and revised rules on the same reserved development responses. That is a development comparison, not a final independent test. Repeated tuning would require fresh evaluation data.

What has been demonstrated?
A real local baseline and a saved parent proposal. The latest review also exercised the comparison flow with mocked inference. Claim a real parent-driven improvement only once the actual comparison is complete and verified.

What is the business model?
Our proposed model is a subscription for managing local workers, with parent usage separate. We would begin through campus groups and local business networks. Pricing and willingness to pay remain unvalidated.
